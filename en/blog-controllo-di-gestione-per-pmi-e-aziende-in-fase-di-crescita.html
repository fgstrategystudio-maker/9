Management control

Management control for SMEs: a practical guide for growing companies

Reading: about 9 minutes · Updated September 2026

In SMEs, management control almost always arrives late. While the company is small, the owner sees everything: they know the customers, they know how much the staff costs, they can sense whether the month has gone well. Then come more people, more products, more channels. At some point revenue grows but peace of mind does not, and simple questions stop having simple answers.

In my work with SMEs, startups and scale-ups I often see the same scene. The numbers are there: invoices, the ERP, bank statements, a few Excel files. What is missing is a common logic and a specific moment when those numbers become decisions. So prices, hiring and investments are discussed on the basis of impressions, and the data comes later, to confirm what had already been decided.

This guide explains what management control for SMEs is, how to set it up step by step without red tape, which margins and costs to read, which indicators to bring to management, how often and which mistakes to avoid. The goal is a lean system that the company actually uses every month.

What management control is and what it does for an SME

Management control is the set of numbers, tools and habits that let you understand how the company is doing while things are happening, not six months later with the annual accounts. The financial statements tell the story of the past, following rules designed for the tax authorities and third parties. Management control looks inside: which customer is profitable, which line absorbs resources, which cost is growing faster than revenue.

In an SME it serves three concrete purposes:

  • Understanding margins: where the company creates value and where it consumes it, by customer, product, channel or job.
  • Managing costs: distinguishing the necessary structure from spending that has lost its connection with the strategy.
  • Choosing priorities: deciding what to push, what to correct and what to stop, starting from a shared basis.

Then there is the question of financial sustainability. Growing revenue does not mean growing in quality. A company can sell more and earn less, because it has taken on customers at prices that are too low, added fixed costs or multiplied its operational complexity. Management control shows you this difference before it hits your cash.

The signs that it is time to start

There is no revenue threshold above which it becomes mandatory. There are signs, and they usually appear before the real problem:

  • revenue grows but at year end profit stays flat or falls;
  • you cannot say with confidence which are your three most profitable customers or products;
  • costs are rising and nobody can really explain why;
  • sales, production and administration bring different numbers on the same topic to meetings;
  • important decisions are made in a hurry, looking mainly at the bank balance.

If you recognize yourself in two or three of these points, the informal model has already reached its limit. You do not need a huge project. You need to start in an orderly way.

How to set up management control in an SME, step by step

The path I follow with clients is almost always the same. The details change, not the sequence.

1. Start from decisions, not from the report

The most common mistake is starting from the format: a report template copied from a larger company, with dozens of line items. I do the opposite. First I list the recurring decisions management has to make: prices, discounts, hiring, marketing budget, investments, customers to develop or to let go. Every number that goes into the system must serve at least one of these decisions. If it serves none, it stays out.

2. Build a management income statement

The management income statement reclassifies costs according to how the company works, not how the accountant records them. The minimum structure is this: revenue, variable costs, contribution margin, direct fixed costs of individual areas, second-level margin, general overhead, operating result. It is the foundation that then lets you read margins by customer, product or line.

3. Choose your dimensions of analysis

Not every company needs to read everything. A services company thinks in terms of jobs and customers. A product company thinks in terms of product family and channel. A business with several locations thinks by outlet. Choose one or two dimensions that truly reflect how the company creates or loses value, and start from those. Others can be added later, if needed.

4. Put your source data in order

This is often where you discover that the data exists but does not connect: different customer codes in the ERP and the CRM, staff costs not allocated, jobs closed without a final cost report. You do not need perfection. You need a clear rule on where each piece of data comes from and who updates it. This step often leads to business process optimization work, because messy data almost always stems from a poorly defined process.

5. Set a budget and targets

A number without a reference point says little. A simple annual budget, broken down by month, lets you read the variance between expected and actual. At the start, a budget covering revenue, contribution margin and the main fixed costs is enough. Detail comes with experience.

6. Set a calendar and an owner

The system stays alive if it has a rhythm. Data closed by a specific date the following month, report ready a few days later, a review meeting with management. And one person accountable for it, internal or external. Without an owner, management control grinds to a halt at the first busy month.

Margins and costs: what to really read

The heart of management control for SMEs is reading margins. Revenue tells you how much you sold. Margin tells you how much you have left to pay for the structure and make a profit.

Contribution margin and break-even point

Contribution margin is the difference between revenue and variable costs. For example, a company with revenue of €2 million and €1.2 million in variable costs has a contribution margin of €800,000, or 40%. If fixed costs are €700,000, the break-even point is found by dividing 700,000 by 0.40: you need €1.75 million in revenue not to lose money.

With this number in hand, many decisions change. Take a 5% discount on a product with a 40% margin. On a €100 price, the margin goes from €40 to €35. To bring home the same margin you need about 14% more volume. Put like that, the discount granted to close a deal quickly looks far less harmless.

Reading costs with judgment

Across-the-board cost cutting almost never works. Sometimes it improves the quarter and worsens quality, speed or sales capacity. It is better to divide costs into four groups:

  • costs that generate competitive advantage, to protect;
  • costs that ensure continuity, to keep under control;
  • costs that have become habit, to question;
  • fixed costs that could become variable or more selective.

This reading changes the conversation. Instead of asking how much to cut, you ask what you are buying with every euro spent. For a closer look at how to read profitability by customer, product and channel and turn it into concrete choices, you can read how to read numbers and margins without deciding on gut feeling.

Indicators, cadence and management reporting

A few well-defined indicators

Management does not need everything. It needs 6 to 10 stable indicators, with a clear definition and an owner. A starting set that works in many SMEs:

  • revenue and variance against budget;
  • contribution margin in value and as a percentage;
  • staff costs as a share of revenue;
  • fixed overhead costs against budget;
  • margin for the chosen dimensions (main customers, lines or channels);
  • average days to collect and projected cash at 90 days;
  • a sales indicator that anticipates revenue, such as orders won or weighted pipeline.

The rest of the data remains available for deeper analysis, but it does not go into the dashboard. Separating the base data, the summary for management and the in-depth analyses keeps the report from becoming a thirty-page document nobody opens. A good rule: the management dashboard should be readable in under twenty minutes. For more on how to choose and visualize indicators, see the guide on business KPIs and dashboards.

The rhythm that makes the system work

Good management control has a simple cadence that is respected. The one I usually propose:

  • weekly: a few operational numbers, such as orders, collections and cash;
  • monthly: management income statement, margins, variances against budget and a review meeting;
  • quarterly: review of forecasts, priorities and, if necessary, the budget.

The monthly meeting should last less than an hour and revolve around four questions. Where are we above or below expectations, and why? Which costs are growing more than planned? Which customers, channels or projects are squeezing margin? Which actions start within the next month, and who is accountable for them?

The last question is the one that makes the difference. If a review does not end with decisions and owners, management control remains a nicely formatted accounting exercise. The following month you start again from the actions agreed: were they carried out? Did they have an effect on the numbers?

A practical example

Let's take a hypothetical case. A B2B services company with €3 million in revenue and 25 people grows by 15% in a year, but profit falls. The owner is convinced the problem is overhead and is thinking about cutting.

We set up a management income statement by customer, allocating staff hours to jobs. The picture that emerges is different. The top five customers account for 45% of revenue, but two of them have a contribution margin of 12%, against a company average of 35%. They ask for constant customizations and tight deadlines, and over the years they have obtained discounts nobody has ever questioned again. Meanwhile a group of mid-sized customers has margins above 45% but receives little sales attention.

The decisions become concrete: renegotiate terms and scope with the two large customers, shift part of the sales effort to the most profitable segment, set a minimum margin threshold for new proposals. No cuts to overhead, which was not the problem. Without a customer-level reading, the company would have pulled the wrong lever.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Copying large-company models. Too many line items, too many cost centers, too much time to update them. In an SME the system must stay essential.
  • Starting from the software. The tool comes after the logic. Many SMEs get off to a good start with a well-structured spreadsheet.
  • Delegating everything to the finance office. Management control works when management uses it and when the heads of the areas that affect margins and costs are involved.
  • Mistaking an unusual month for a trend. It is better to compare several periods and look at the trend over the last three or six months.
  • Allocating overhead arbitrarily. A forced allocation can make a product look loss-making when it actually helps cover fixed costs. Read the contribution margin first, then the rest.
  • Not closing the loop. Reports without actions, actions without owners, owners without a check the following month.

Where to start

Management control for SMEs does not require a fully fledged finance department. It requires clear logic, a few reliable numbers and a reading rhythm that is respected. You can start with outside support that designs the framework and then bring it in-house as the company grows.

FAQ

Is management control useful for a small business too?

Yes. In SMEs it is often even more useful, because resources are limited and a wrong decision on prices, customers or hiring weighs more. An essential framework, proportionate to the size of the company, is enough.

Do I need an in-house controller to get started?

Not always. You can start with an external consultant who sets up the management income statement, indicators and review routine, and then bring the system in-house as the company grows.

How long does it take to see the first results?

It depends on the quality of the starting data, but a few months are generally enough to have a reliable management income statement and the first readings of margins useful for decision-making.

Do I need specific software?

No, at least not at the start. The management logic comes first. A well-structured spreadsheet is enough to get going; more advanced tools make sense when data sources and users increase.
