Sales enablement for SMEs: what salespeople really need

Reading: about 8 minutes · Updated September 2026

Sales enablement is one of those phrases that trigger two opposite reactions in SMEs. Some think it is something for large corporations with dedicated teams, others reduce it to "redoing the sales slides". Either way, the point is missed. Sales enablement is simply everything the company provides to its salespeople so that they spend more time selling well and less time searching, improvising or reinventing.

In my work I often see sales teams made up of capable people who each work with their own materials, their own answers to objections, their own version of the value proposition. Each has built their own kit over time. The result is that the quality of the sale depends on who meets the customer, and that a new salesperson has to rebuild everything from scratch.

In this article I start from a practical question: what does a salesperson really need to sell better? Content, training, tools, and marketing's contribution. Then we look at how to measure whether enablement is working and how to get started in an SME without creating a new department.

What sales enablement means in practice

If the sales process defines what happens at each stage, enablement defines what the salesperson tackles each stage with. They are two different and complementary things. A clear process without tools leaves the team empty-handed. Excellent tools without a process, as I explain in the article on how to structure a B2B sales process, end up being used at random.

In concrete terms, sales enablement rests on four components:

  • Content: everything the salesperson uses or sends to the customer, from case studies to proposal templates.
  • Training: onboarding new hires, updates on product and market, practice on selling skills.
  • Tools: CRM, templates, calculators, content libraries, anything that reduces repetitive work.
  • Coaching: discussion of real deals, which turns what is learned into habit.

In an SME these four components do not require a dedicated manager. They require someone who looks after them consistently, a few hours a week, and a clear criterion for deciding what to do first.

The content that really matters, stage by stage

The most useful way to think about sales content is to associate it with a stage of the process and a specific moment in the deal. Content nobody knows when to use will not be used.

For qualification and discovery

Here what you need most are tools for the salesperson, not for the customer: a question guide for the first call, a description of the ideal customer with the signals for recognizing them, a short sheet for each industry served with the typical problems and the language those customers use.

For the proposal

A proposal template that starts from the customer's problem, two or three one-page stories of similar customers and, where it makes sense, a tool for estimating costs and benefits. The template is not there to standardize the offer. It is there to let the salesperson focus on the customized part instead of the layout.

For objections and closing

A collection of the most frequent objections with the answers that work best, written starting from real deals. Answers to recurring technical and contractual questions. Materials designed for the people inside the customer's company who have to approve the decision without having taken part in the calls: that is often where deals stall.

A rule I often apply: every piece of content must have an owner and a review date. And every six months, the ones nobody has used are removed. A small, up-to-date library is worth far more than a folder full of old presentations nobody trusts.

Where to keep content and tools

Useful content that a salesperson cannot find in thirty seconds, during or right after a call, is almost as good as content that does not exist. That is why the question "where do we keep it?" matters as much as "what do we produce?". An SME does not need a dedicated platform. It needs a single shared place, organized by process stage rather than by the department that produced the material, and linked to the CRM: from an opportunity record at the proposal stage, the salesperson should be one click away from the proposal template and the relevant case studies.

The same goes for other tools. Email templates for recurring steps, a calculator for standard quotes, automatic follow-up reminders: small things that, added together, give back hours every week. The criterion for choosing them is simple. Ask the team which activities they repeat most often without adding value for the customer, and start from those.

Training and onboarding new salespeople

Sales training in SMEs tends to focus on the product. The new salesperson spends the first weeks learning features and price lists, then gets sent out to customers. But what they lack most is not product knowledge. It is customer knowledge: what problems customers have, how they talk about them, what makes them hesitate, how they decide.

A good onboarding path alternates three things:

  • Listening: the new hire sits in on real calls with experienced colleagues, or listens to recordings, with a guide to what to look for.
  • Practice: discovery and objection-handling role plays before doing it with real customers.
  • Shadowing and review: the first deals they run on their own are reviewed together, stage by stage.

Training does not end with onboarding, though. The most effective way to continue, and also the cheapest, is a regular deal review. You take one won deal and one lost deal, and the team discusses what worked and what did not. The best content also comes out of these discussions, because it starts from real situations.

The role of marketing

In many companies marketing and sales work on separate tracks. Marketing produces content designed to attract leads, sales uses materials it has built on its own. The customer, meanwhile, hears two different stories about the same company.

In sales enablement marketing plays a central role: it is the function that knows how to write, lay out, tell customer stories and keep the message consistent. But it must work at the request of sales, not on assumptions. The mechanism that works best is a simple cycle:

  • salespeople flag questions, objections and situations where material is missing;
  • marketing produces the content, shares it and explains when to use it;
  • after a few weeks, both check together whether it is being used and whether it helps;
  • what works becomes standard, the rest is corrected or removed.

For this cycle to work, the two departments need shared goals and numbers. I go into this in more depth in the article on how to align marketing, sales, data and business goals. When marketing is also judged on the quality of the opportunities that reach sales, enablement naturally becomes part of its work.

How to measure sales enablement

Enablement is hard to measure directly, because its effect passes through people. But that does not mean it should be done on trust. There are concrete indicators to watch before and after each intervention:

  • Ramp-up time: how long it takes a new salesperson to close their first contract on their own.
  • Conversion by stage: if you have introduced a discovery guide, the discovery-to-proposal rate should improve.
  • Cycle length: better materials for approvers should shorten the time between proposal and signature.
  • Content usage: which materials are used, by whom and at what stage.
  • Spread between salespeople: if enablement works, the gap between the results of the best performers and the rest tends to narrow.

The practical advice is to link every intervention to an indicator before carrying it out. "Let's redo the proposal template" becomes "let's redo the proposal template to improve the close rate on proposals, which is our weak point today". That way, after three months, you know whether it worked.

How to get started in an SME: a practical example

Take a hypothetical company that distributes industrial components, with four salespeople and one person handling marketing. Each salesperson has their own way of presenting the company, proposals are a different spreadsheet for each of them, and the most recent hire took almost a year to become self-sufficient.

A ninety-day plan could look like this. In the first four weeks you collect the materials each salesperson really uses, listen to a few calls and identify the five most frequent objections. In the following four weeks marketing, together with the most experienced salesperson, produces three things: a discovery guide, a single proposal template and a collection of answers to objections. In the last month the team works with the new materials, and a fortnightly review of one won deal and one lost deal is introduced.

No new software, no consultants in a classroom for days. Three tools built on the team's real problems and a habit of discussion. It is a modest starting point, and that is exactly why it lasts.

Mistakes to avoid and first steps

The mistakes I see most often in enablement projects share a common root: starting from tools instead of problems.

  • Producing beautiful, useless materials. A polished presentation that does not answer customers' questions stays in the folder.
  • Buying a platform before having content. The tool organizes what is there, it does not create it.
  • One-off training. Two days of classroom training without practice and follow-up review leave little behind.
  • Leaving salespeople out. Materials imposed from above get ignored or rewritten in private.
  • Not measuring. Without a linked indicator, you will never know what worked.

The first step is to ask your salespeople which three situations they feel least equipped for. Their answers will tell you where to start better than any theoretical model. FAQ

Is sales enablement useful for a team with just a few salespeople?

Yes. In a small team each person weighs heavily on results, and shared materials, good onboarding and regular reviews reduce dependence on individuals.

Who should be responsible for sales enablement in an SME?

You do not need a dedicated role. What usually works well is a collaboration between the sales manager, who sets the priorities, and marketing, which produces the content.

How can you tell whether sales enablement is working?

By linking every intervention to an indicator: ramp-up time for new hires, conversion at the stage concerned, cycle length, actual use of materials. You compare the values before and after the intervention.

Where is it best to start?

From the situations in which salespeople feel least equipped. Often a discovery guide, a common proposal template and a collection of answers to the most frequent objections are enough.
