SEO articles for businesses: how to write them to win customers

Reading: about 9 minutes · Updated September 2026

Many companies start a blog with a simple idea: write a few articles, show up on Google, get leads. Six months later the picture is almost always the same. Twenty or so articles published, a few hundred visits a month, not a single inquiry that can be traced back to the blog. And the hasty conclusion that "SEO doesn't work in our industry".

In my work with SMEs and startups I find that the problem is rarely SEO itself. The problem is that articles are treated as text to fill a page, not as sales tools. SEO articles for businesses work when they do three things at once: they rank for a search your ideal customer really makes, they demonstrate expertise the reader recognizes and they guide the reader towards a next step. If one of these is missing, the article becomes a cost.

In this guide I set out the method I use with clients: how to read search intent, how to choose keywords, how to structure the text, how to build authority, how to link the blog to your service pages, how to measure results and when it makes sense to outsource the writing.

Start from search intent, not volume

Every search comes from a need. Before asking how many people search for a phrase, ask why they search for it. This is the difference between generic traffic and qualified traffic: a visitor is qualified when their problem, their level of awareness and their context match what your company can solve.

To simplify, the searches that matter to a business fall into four families:

  • Informational: the person wants to understand a problem ("how to calculate customer acquisition cost").
  • Commercial investigation: they are comparing approaches or solutions ("marketing consultant or agency").
  • Transactional: they are looking for a supplier ("marketing consulting Rome").
  • Navigational: they are looking for a specific brand or website. Here an article has little to gain.

Blog articles work mainly on the first two families. Transactional searches usually belong to service pages. A common mistake is writing an article for a transactional query: Google shows supplier pages and your piece stays on page two, however good it is.

The simplest check is also the most useful. Search the phrase on Google and look at the top ten results. If you find guides, the intent is informational. If you find agency and consultant websites, it is transactional. If you find comparisons and lists, it is commercial investigation. Write the type of content Google is already rewarding, but do it more clearly, more concretely and more completely.

How to choose the right keywords for your business

Keyword selection is the moment when much of the result is decided. With clients I apply three filters, in this order.

1. Closeness to the service

The question is: could someone searching for this phrase buy what I sell, today or in six months? A query like "what is marketing" brings volume but a scattered audience. A query like "how to measure the return on online campaigns" brings fewer people, much closer to a problem a services business can solve.

2. A realistic chance of ranking

A young website is unlikely to outrank large portals on generic terms. It is better to start from more specific searches, often four or five words long, where competition is weaker and intent is clearer. You move up to broader terms after building a cluster of solid content on the same topic.

3. Expertise to demonstrate

Choose topics on which you have something to say that others do not: cases, numbers, mistakes seen in the field. If you have no point of view on a topic, the article will be a variation on those already on page one, and Google has no reason to prefer it.

Each article should have one main keyword and a small group of related variants and questions. Two articles targeting the same search compete with each other: one complete piece is better than three similar ones. To spread topics over time and tie them to your services, you then need an editorial plan for your company blog, which turns the keyword list into a sensible calendar.

The structure of an article people actually read

Someone arriving from Google decides within seconds whether to stay. The structure must tell them immediately that they are in the right place and let them find what they are looking for even when skimming. The one I use most often is this:

  1. A title that contains the keyword and promises a concrete result.
  2. A short introduction that describes the problem as the reader experiences it and says what they will find.
  3. H2 sections that follow the real questions, in the order the reader asks them.
  4. A practical example with numbers, even hypothetical ones, that makes the reasoning applicable.
  5. Common mistakes or selection criteria, which are often the most-read part.
  6. A conclusion that proposes a next step consistent with what has just been read.
  7. FAQs with the secondary questions that do not deserve a full section.

A few writing rules that make the difference: paragraphs of two or three sentences, one idea per paragraph, bold only for key concepts, no generic preambles about the changing market. The right length is whatever it takes to answer completely. An 800-word article that solves the problem beats a 3,000-word one that circles around the topic.

Experience and authority: the advantage that cannot be copied

Today producing a correct text costs next to nothing. That is exactly why a correct text is no longer enough. What sets a strong business article apart is the proof that behind it there is someone who really does that work. Readers, especially in B2B, use content to answer an unspoken question: does this company know how to bring order to the problem I am trying to solve?

There are concrete ways to make experience visible:

  • explain how you tackle a problem, step by step, with your own method;
  • cite real results, even small ones, without inflating them;
  • describe the mistakes you see most often and how you fix them;
  • take a stand when you think a widespread approach does not work;
  • sign the article with a name and a verifiable profile.

An example from my own work: when I write about online campaigns I can explain how, with a financial company, we increased campaign ROI by 110% in three months. A figure like that, together with the reasoning behind it, is worth more than ten paragraphs of theory.

There is also a long-term effect. When several articles cover related topics with the same language and the same thesis, the website starts to convey a way of thinking. Readers perceive method, and this strengthens brand positioning as well as rankings. A neutral blog informs. A blog with a point of view gets remembered.

Internal links to your service pages

Internal links are the most neglected part of the work and one of the most profitable. Google needs them to understand the relationship between pages and which ones matter most. Readers need them to continue their journey instead of going back to the search results.

The rules I apply to every article are few:

  • at least one link to the service page closest to the topic, placed at the point where the reader feels the problem;
  • one or two links to articles in the same topic cluster;
  • descriptive anchor text that says where the link goes, never "click here";
  • from the service page, a link back to the most useful articles.

For example, an article on how to measure content ROI should lead to the marketing consulting page, because people asking that question often need to rethink their entire acquisition system. If your audience is mainly local, it can also make sense to link to a location-based service page, such as the one for marketing consulting services. That way the blog becomes a network that brings readers and relevance to the pages that generate leads.

How to measure results, with a practical example

The number of visits is the most visible indicator and the least useful if looked at on its own. An article with 3,000 visits a month and no leads is worth less than one with 200 visits that brings in two qualified inquiries.

The indicators to track

  • Visibility: impressions and average position on target queries, from Google Search Console.
  • Engagement: reading time, scroll depth, clicks through to other articles.
  • Journey: how many sessions go from the article to a service page.
  • Business: contact requests originating from the blog and how many become real opportunities.

The last item requires discipline: recording the landing page for every lead and carrying it over into the CRM. Without this step the blog will forever remain a cost line that is hard to justify. If you want to connect content, leads and revenue in a single picture, a management control system for marketing and sales stops you deciding on gut feeling. Remember timing too: a new article often takes three to six months to settle, so it is better to assess by quarter rather than by week.

A practical example

Let's take a hypothetical case. A company that sells practice management software for professional firms has been publishing two articles a month for a year on generic topics: productivity, digitalization, tips for remote working. The blog gets 4,000 visits a month and brings in about one demo request every two months.

After an analysis, the plan becomes this: merge the eight productivity articles into a single comprehensive guide, write six new articles on problems specific to the target audience (for example "how to manage deadlines for 200 clients without Excel spreadsheets"), add a link in each piece to the most relevant feature and track every inquiry with its source page.

In a scenario like this it is realistic for total traffic to stay similar or drop a little, while the share of readers close to buying goes up. If inquiries go from one every two months to three a month, the blog has changed its role despite getting fewer visits. It is the same principle I applied with a proptech startup that went from 0 to 40 B2B customers in six months: what counts is the right people, not the crowd.

Mistakes to avoid and when to outsource the writing

The most common mistakes

  • Writing for everyone: an article that speaks to everyone convinces no one.
  • Chasing high-volume keywords that are far from your service.
  • Publishing near-identical articles on the same topic, which compete with each other.
  • Using a neutral, textbook tone, with no point of view.
  • Leaving articles without links to your service pages.
  • Measuring visits and never leads.
  • Publishing and forgetting: the best pieces should be updated at least once a year.

When it pays to outsource the writing

Not every company needs to outsource. If you have someone in-house who knows the market, writes well and has time on an ongoing basis, it may be worth keeping the writing internal and getting help only with method, topic selection and editing.

Handing the writing to an outside professional makes sense when the blog has stalled or comes out in fits and starts, when the articles are there but bring in no leads and nobody can explain why, when the people who know the subject (you, a partner, a technical expert) have no time to write, or when nobody is holding together SEO, positioning and sales goals.

Choose your writer carefully. A good content partner asks you questions about the business before proposing titles, interviews you to draw out your experience and explains how they will measure results. Be wary of anyone selling packages by number of articles without asking who your customers are. In B2B, where sales cycles are long and readers are demanding, this difference weighs even more.

SEO articles for businesses are one of the longest-lasting marketing investments, provided you treat them as a sales tool. FAQ

How many SEO articles should a company publish?

There is no right number. For an SME, two to four solid articles a month linked to its services perform better than ten generic pieces. Consistency over time matters more than volume.

How long does it take for an SEO article to deliver results?

It usually takes three to six months for an article to settle in the search results. That is why it is best to assess the blog quarterly, looking at the leads generated as well.

Is it better to target generic or specific keywords?

For most companies it pays to start with specific searches, closer to the service and less contested. They bring fewer visits but more qualified readers.

Are SEO articles still useful now that AI generates answers?

Yes, if they offer experience, examples and points of view that an automated summary cannot provide. Generic content loses clicks, while distinctive content keeps building trust and leads.
