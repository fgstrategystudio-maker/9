How to increase B2B sales by improving your pipeline

Reading: about 8 minutes · Updated September 2026

When a business owner asks me how to increase B2B sales, the first answer already in their head is almost always the same: we need more leads. More contacts, more campaigns, more calls. Sometimes that is true. Far more often, though, the company already has enough opportunities and loses some of them along the way, at specific points nobody is looking at.

The place where these points become visible is the pipeline. Not the total at the bottom of the monthly report, but the pipeline read stage by stage: how many opportunities come in, how many move on to the next stage, how long they sit still, how many drop out and why. Improving pipeline conversion means working on these steps one at a time, starting with the one that costs the most today.

In this article I show you the method I use with clients: how to read the pipeline, where opportunities are usually lost, how to work on qualification, follow-up, proposals and negotiation, and how to build credible forecasts. With a numerical example that shows why it often pays to fix a stage before buying more traffic.

Read the pipeline by stage, not as a total

Most companies look at the pipeline as a single number: "we have €400,000 in play". It is a reassuring figure but it says almost nothing. It does not tell you how much of that value is real, how much has been stuck for months, how much will die at the proposal stage.

To really read it you need simply defined stages. In a typical B2B company five steps are enough: qualified opportunity, discovery, proposal sent, negotiation, close (won or lost). For each stage I always look at four numbers:

  • Volume: how many opportunities entered the stage in the period.
  • Conversion rate: how many moved on to the next stage.
  • Time in stage: how many days on average they stay in that stage.
  • Exit reason: why the lost ones were lost.

With these four numbers per stage, the question "how do we sell more?" becomes much more concrete. It becomes "why do only three out of ten discovery calls lead to a proposal?" or "why do proposals stay open twice as long as a year ago?". Those are questions you can answer.

Where opportunities are usually lost

Every company has its own main leak, but the critical points repeat themselves. Between first contact and discovery you lose those who were not really a fit or who were followed up too late. Between discovery and proposal you lose opportunities where the need was not fully understood, and the salesperson sends a proposal anyway "to see what happens". Between proposal and close you lose on price, on competition, but above all to no decision: the customer does not say no, they simply say nothing.

This last category is the one companies underestimate most. In the CRM the deals stay open, the pipeline value looks high, but in reality they are opportunities already lost. If you do not close them as lost, they distort every number you will use to decide.

Qualification: the most underestimated lever

If I had to name a single intervention with the best effort-to-result ratio, I would say qualification. A salesperson who spends half their time on opportunities that will never close does not have a commitment problem. They have a filtering problem.

Qualifying well means checking a few things before investing time in in-depth discovery and tailored proposals:

  • the customer has a problem your offer solves, and recognizes it as such;
  • that problem is a priority now, not "sooner or later";
  • there is a budget, or at least an order of magnitude compatible with your price;
  • you know who decides and who influences the decision;
  • there is a date or event that makes the decision necessary.

You do not need a complicated framework. You need these questions to be asked every time, the answers to end up in the CRM and an opportunity without answers not to move forward. The initial result is often alarming: the pipeline shrinks. But what remains converts better, and the team stops wasting energy.

Qualifying also means having the courage to disqualify. Telling a prospect "we are not the right solution for you today" frees up time for those who are a fit. In my work I often find that the best-organized companies are the ones that close far more opportunities as lost, and much earlier.

Follow-up, proposal and negotiation

Once the opportunity is qualified, conversion depends on three moments in which the salesperson can make the difference or let things take their course.

Follow-up needs a date and a reason

Weak follow-up has a recognizable form: "I'm writing to see whether you've had a chance to look at the proposal". It adds nothing and puts the customer in the position of having to reply without any reason to. Useful follow-up brings something new: an answer to a doubt that came up on the call, a relevant example, a clarification on start-up times. And every open opportunity should always have a next step with a date agreed with the customer, not decided by the salesperson alone.

The proposal must reflect the discovery

Many B2B proposals all look alike: company presentation, list of services, price. A proposal that converts starts from the customer's problem in their own words, explains what will change and how quickly, and only then gets to the price. If the salesperson cannot write the first lines of the proposal with information specific to that customer, discovery was not finished. Better to have another call than to send a generic document.

In negotiation every concession has a price

A discount granted straight away, without asking for anything in return, teaches the customer that the price was inflated. In negotiation it pays to decide in advance what can be conceded and what you ask for in return: a longer contract term, advance payment, a fast start, a case study. That way the negotiation stays a negotiation and does not turn into a race to the bottom.

Sales forecasts management can actually use

A pipeline read by stage lets you make better forecasts. The most common forecast in SMEs is the "gut feeling" kind: the salesperson says a contract will close at the end of the month, and expected revenue is built by adding up these feelings. The problem is that optimism is not evenly distributed among people.

A more solid method uses historical conversion rates. If over the last twelve months 30% of the proposals sent became contracts, the value of open proposals should be weighted by that percentage, not added up in full. On top of this come two simple adjustments: an opportunity stuck in the same stage for more than twice the average time loses weight, and an opportunity with no dated next step does not go into the forecast.

To keep all this readable without rebuilding it by hand every month, a marketing and sales KPI dashboard showing conversion and time per stage helps a lot. But the dashboard comes afterwards: first you need clear stages and data entered with the same rules for everyone.

A numerical example: more leads or better conversion?

Take a hypothetical company selling B2B services with an average contract value of €12,000. In one quarter it generates 100 qualified opportunities. Its funnel today looks like this:

  • 100 opportunities, of which 50 reach discovery (50%);
  • 50 discovery calls, which produce 20 proposals (40%);
  • 20 proposals, of which 5 become contracts (25%);
  • revenue for the quarter: 5 contracts at €12,000, i.e. €60,000.

Scenario A, more leads. The company invests to take opportunities from 100 to 120, all else being equal. It gets 60 discovery calls, 24 proposals, 6 contracts: €72,000. A 20% increase, paid for with marketing budget and more team hours, because every stage has more volume to handle.

Scenario B, better conversion. The company leaves leads unchanged and works on two steps. It improves discovery and qualification, and the discovery-to-proposal rate rises from 40% to 50%: proposals go up to 25. Then it works on proposals and follow-up, and the close rate on proposals rises from 25% to 35%. The result is about 8 to 9 contracts, i.e. between €96,000 and €108,000.

The numbers are hypothetical, but the logic almost always holds: conversion improvements multiply along the stages, while an increase in leads adds up and brings more costs with it. This does not mean demand generation is not needed. It means it pays to grow it on top of a process that does not waste what comes in.

Mistakes to avoid

With clients I often see the same mistakes, regardless of industry and company size:

  • Looking only at final revenue. The closing figure arrives late and does not tell you where to intervene.
  • Keeping dead opportunities open. They inflate the pipeline and make forecasts useless.
  • Stages defined by the salesperson's activity. "I sent the proposal" is not progress if the customer has not confirmed interest and timing.
  • Changing everything at once. If you work on five levers in the same month you will not know which one worked.
  • Blaming the team. When every salesperson loses at the same point, the problem is in the process or the offer, not in the people.
  • Not recording loss reasons. Without reasons, every lost deal is a wasted learning opportunity.

Where to start

The first step does not require new tools. Take the opportunities from the last six or twelve months, assign them to clear stages, calculate conversion and time for each. In a few hours of work you will see where the main leak is concentrated. That is the stage to work on first.

From there the work broadens: defining shared exit criteria, as I explain in the article on how to structure a B2B sales process, and giving salespeople materials and training suited to each stage, which is the subject of sales enablement. Every improved stage makes everything you invest upstream more productive.

FAQ

Do you need more leads to increase B2B sales?

Not always. If the pipeline loses many opportunities at one stage, improving that step pays off more than increasing incoming volume, because conversion improvements multiply along the stages.

Which numbers should you look at to improve pipeline conversion?

For each stage: how many opportunities come in, how many move on to the next stage, how long they sit still and why they are lost. These four figures are enough to identify the main bottleneck.

How can sales forecasts be made more reliable?

By weighting the value of open opportunities with the historical conversion rates of each stage, instead of adding up salespeople's estimates. Deals stuck for too long or with no dated next step should be excluded or scaled down.

How long does it take to see results?

It depends on the length of the sales cycle. Generally, work on qualification and follow-up shows up in the stage numbers within one or two cycles, while revenue moves with some delay.
