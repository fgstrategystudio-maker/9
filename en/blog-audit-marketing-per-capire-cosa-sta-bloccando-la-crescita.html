Marketing audit: how to find out what is holding back growth

Reading: about 9 minutes · Updated September 2026

When a business owner calls me to talk about marketing, they don't usually say "I need an audit". They tell me the campaigns aren't performing like they used to, that leads come in but don't close, that the website was rebuilt and nothing changed, or that they spend a significant amount every month and can't say for sure what is working. These are all symptoms. The cause, almost always, lies somewhere else.

A marketing audit exists precisely for this: to separate symptoms from causes before spending more time and more budget. It is a structured analysis of how the company attracts, persuades and converts customers, carried out with an outside perspective and a precise goal. Understand what is holding back growth and decide in what order to act.

In this article I explain what a business marketing audit is, when it makes sense to do one, what needs to be analyzed, how I work and what you should have in hand at the end. You will also find a practical example and the mistakes I see most often.

What a marketing audit is and what it is not

A marketing audit is a reasoned snapshot of the system through which the company generates demand and turns it into revenue. It doesn't stop at individual tools. It looks at how positioning, channels, messaging, funnel, data and people fit together, and looks for the point where the system loses energy.

It is worth clarifying straight away what it is not. It is not an automated report generated by software that gives your website a score. It is not a campaign review done by the people who run the campaigns, because whoever manages a channel tends to defend it. And it is not a list of a hundred things to improve, all with the same priority. A good audit produces a few clear conclusions, ranked by impact.

That is exactly the difference from a simple marketing analysis. An analysis describes what is happening. An audit reaches a judgment: what to keep, what to fix, what to stop. If you want to see how I set up this work in detail, you will find the full description on the page about the marketing and business audit.

Marketing audit vs business audit: what is the difference

The two are often confused. A marketing audit focuses on how the company generates demand and converts it. A business and process audit looks at the whole machine: revenue model, margins, organization, operating processes, customer management after the sale.

How do you choose? If the main problem is that too few customers come in, or the wrong ones, start with marketing. If customers come in but margins are getting thinner, delivery times are getting longer or the organization is struggling to keep up with growth, the problem lies further upstream and a business audit makes more sense. In some cases a marketing audit uncovers a problem that isn't about marketing at all, for example a price that doesn't hold up or a service with too much churn. When that happens I say so openly, because working on marketing in that situation would mean pouring customers into a leaky bucket.

When you need a marketing audit

You don't need to do one every year out of habit. You need one when there is a disconnect between effort and results, or when the company is about to enter a new phase and doesn't want to carry over the mistakes of the previous one. In my work, the most typical moments are these:

  • Growth has stalled and nobody can explain exactly why.
  • The cost of acquiring a customer has been rising for months, while the average customer value stays flat.
  • Sales complains about poorly qualified leads and marketing replies that the lead numbers are good.
  • You are about to launch a new product, enter a new market or hire someone dedicated to marketing.
  • You work with two or three different suppliers and each one tells a different story about the results.
  • The budget has been increased, but results have not grown in the same proportion.

Then there is a less obvious case. Things are going well, but nobody knows why. It is a more fragile situation than it looks: if you don't know the levers that got you here, you don't know what to protect when the market changes.

What a business marketing audit analyzes

A well-done business marketing audit looks at six areas. I treat them separately for clarity, but the value lies in reading them together, because the problem in one area almost always originates in the one next to it.

Positioning

Who you are targeting, for which problem, and why they should choose you over an alternative. It sounds obvious, yet it is the area where I find the most confusion. Companies that claim to serve "SMEs in every sector" or that describe their offer with the same words as their competitors. If positioning is vague, every channel is working uphill.

Channels

Which channels bring in real customers, beyond leads and visits. For each one I look at cost, volume, quality and dependency: a company that gets almost all its customers from a single source has a risk that at the very least needs to be made visible.

Funnel

The path from first contact to signature, with the conversion rates between one stage and the next. This is usually where you find the bottleneck: the point where most people drop off.

Messaging

Website, landing pages, ads, sales materials. I check whether they say the same thing, whether they talk about the customer's problem or only about the company, and whether they are consistent with what the sales team says in person.

Data and measurement

What is tracked, how far the numbers can be trusted, and whether there is a link between marketing data and sales data. I often find that the numbers used to make important decisions are incomplete or calculated in different ways by different people.

Team and decision-making

Who does what, who decides, with what information and how often. Many marketing problems are actually governance problems: nobody has the authority to say no to an initiative, or decisions are made on gut feeling during a meeting and never checked.

The method: how I work during an audit

The four phases

The method I use is simple to describe and takes discipline to apply. It has four phases.

  1. Listening. I talk to the people who run the company, the people who handle marketing and the people who sell. Their versions are often different, and the differences are already valuable information.
  2. Data collection. Campaign history, website data, CRM, revenue by customer and by channel when available. I work with what is there, flagging where data is missing or unreliable.
  3. Diagnosis. I bring the six areas together and look for causes, not symptoms. For each problem I ask myself: if we solved this, would it really change anything in revenue?
  4. Priorities. I rank the actions by expected impact and required effort, and turn them into a plan for the next 90 days.

One point I insist on: the audit ends with a conversation, not with a document sent by email. The conclusions need to be discussed with the people who have to make the decisions, otherwise they stay in a file nobody opens again.

What you get at the end

At the end of an audit you should have a few very concrete things in hand:

  • A two- or three-page summary of the main problems, explained in a way that someone who doesn't work in marketing can understand.
  • The main bottleneck identified, that is, the point to act on first.
  • A list of activities to stop, because they consume resources without delivering results.
  • A 90-day plan with owners and timelines.
  • A small set of indicators to tell whether the actions are working, chosen with the criteria I describe in the article on how to choose the right KPIs.

The item that surprises clients most is almost always the list of things to stop. Freeing up budget and attention from useless activities is often the quickest win of the whole project.

A practical example

Let's take a hypothetical case. A B2B services company, with revenue of around €2 million, invests about €8,000 a month in online advertising and an agency. It generates 120 leads a month. The owner is convinced the problem is quantity and wants to increase the budget.

The audit tells a different story. Of the 120 leads, the salesperson considers about 30 a good fit. Of these, 20 get to a first call and 4 become customers. The bottleneck is not at the top of the funnel: it is the step from lead to qualified lead, where 75% of people are lost. Looking at the messaging, the reason becomes clear. The website and the ads talk generically about "solutions for businesses", so they also attract companies that are too small or off target.

In this scenario, increasing the budget would have brought in more wrong leads and more wasted time for the salesperson. The priorities become different: redefine the ideal customer, rewrite the main pages and ads to speak to that customer, add two qualifying questions to the contact form. The goal for the next 90 days is not more leads, but to take qualified leads from 30 to 45 with the same spend. If that works, with the same close rate, customers per month go from 4 to 6.

It is a simplified example, but the dynamic is very common. The perceived problem and the real problem rarely coincide.

Mistakes to avoid

These are the mistakes I see most often, both when the audit is done internally and when it is outsourced:

  • Having it done by the people who run the channels. Someone paid to make a channel work is unlikely to conclude that the channel should be scaled back.
  • Looking only at marketing data. Without sales data you don't know whether leads become customers, so you don't know anything useful.
  • Confusing volume with quality. More visits and more leads are not a result if they don't translate into revenue.
  • Coming out with too many priorities. If the audit points to fifteen urgent actions, in practice it points to none.
  • Not involving sales. The people who talk to customers every day know things no report shows.
  • Stopping at the diagnosis. An audit without a plan and without owners remains an intellectual exercise.

Where to start

If you recognize yourself in any of the situations described, the first step is an honest marketing analysis of where you stand today, even on your own. Try answering three questions: which channels do your customers really come from, not just your leads? At what point in the journey do most people drop off? Who, in your company, decides what to do and what to stop doing in marketing?

If you can't answer precisely, or if the answers change depending on who you ask, that is the sign a marketing audit could be useful to you. It is the work I start most marketing consulting projects with, because it lets you invest time and budget where there is a real return. FAQ

How long does a marketing audit take?

It depends on the complexity of the company and the availability of data. For an SME a few weeks are usually enough, including interviews, analysis of the numbers and the final presentation of findings.

Do you need perfect data to do a marketing audit?

No. You work with the data available and flag where it is missing or unreliable. Finding out that the numbers can't be trusted is already a useful result.

Can I do a marketing audit internally?

You can do a first marketing analysis on your own by answering the right questions honestly. An outside perspective, however, helps you move past entrenched beliefs and get marketing and sales on the same page.

What is the difference between a marketing audit and a business audit?

A marketing audit analyzes how the company generates and converts demand. A business audit looks at the whole organization: revenue model, margins, processes and structure.
