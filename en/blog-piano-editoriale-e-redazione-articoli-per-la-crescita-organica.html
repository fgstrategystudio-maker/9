Company blog editorial plan: how to build it around your services

Reading: about 8 minutes · Updated September 2026

When a company tells me it has an editorial plan, it almost always shows me a spreadsheet with dates and titles. Three articles in March, three in April, a few seasonal occasions. The calendar is there. What is missing is the answer to a simple question: why these topics in particular, and what are they supposed to achieve?

An editorial plan for a company blog becomes useful when it stops being a list of titles and works like a map. A map that starts from the services you sell, follows the journey your customer takes before buying and spreads content over time according to business priorities and real resources.

In this article I show you how to build it step by step: how to draw topics from your services, how to cover every stage of the customer journey, how to turn it all into a sustainable calendar and how to tell whether it is working.

An editorial plan and a calendar are not the same thing

The calendar tells you when you publish. The editorial plan tells you why you publish, for whom and with what purpose. You can stick to a calendar perfectly and still have a blog that brings in nothing, because consistency alone does not guarantee growth.

A complete editorial plan answers at least these questions:

  • which services or products we want to support with content, and in what order of priority;
  • who the reader is that we want to attract and what problems they have;
  • which topics we cover and which we deliberately leave out;
  • what role each article plays: attracting, persuading, helping to choose;
  • how many resources we really have for writing, editing and updating.

Only after answering these questions does it make sense to open the calendar.

Start from your services: the topic map

The most solid starting point is the list of what you sell. Each important service or product line becomes a topic area, and each topic area has a two-level structure.

The pillar content

Each area needs a broad, comprehensive article covering the main topic. It is the piece that targets the most important keyword in the cluster and links directly to the service page. For a consultant, for example, a pillar might be a complete guide to SEO articles for businesses, linked to the marketing consulting page.

Supporting articles

Around the pillar revolve more specific articles, each on a precise question: a sub-problem, a recurring mistake, a comparison, a use case. Each supporting article links back to the pillar and, where it makes sense, to the service page. The pillar in turn links to the supporting articles.

This structure does two things. It helps Google understand that the site covers a topic in depth, and it helps readers move between pieces of content without getting lost. A site with four well-built topic areas, each with a pillar and six to eight supporting articles, is much stronger than a site with fifty scattered articles.

A practical tip: do not open all the areas at once. Start with the one tied to the service with the highest margin or with the sales cycle best suited to the organic channel, complete it, and only then move on to the next.

Follow the customer journey

The second dimension of the plan is where the reader is. Someone who has just discovered they have a problem looks for different things from someone comparing three suppliers. A blog that speaks to only one of the two leaves half the journey uncovered.

For each topic area it is worth planning content for four stages:

  • Problem awareness: the reader feels a symptom and tries to understand it ("why do campaigns keep costing more and bringing in fewer leads").
  • Solution exploration: they know what they want to solve and are looking for possible routes ("how to improve a landing page's conversion rate").
  • Decision: they compare approaches and suppliers ("outside consultant or in-house hire for marketing").
  • Post-purchase: they are already a customer and want to get more out of what they bought.

The first two stages bring the most traffic. The third brings fewer visits but readers very close to getting in touch, and it is often the one companies neglect for fear of seeming too salesy. An honest article on how to choose a supplier, with clear criteria, is one of the best-converting pieces of content in B2B. The fourth stage supports customer retention and gives sales material to send to clients.

A good exercise is to build a table with topic areas in the rows and stages in the columns. Every empty cell is an opportunity. Every cell with three similar articles is waste to be merged.

Where to find your customers' real questions

Keyword tools tell you how often a phrase is searched, but not which doubts are holding up a deal. That is why the best sources for topics are internal: the questions that come in by email, the objections salespeople hear on first calls, the reasons a proposal is lost. With clients I often run a one-hour session with the people who sell: it almost always produces ten or fifteen topics no tool would have suggested, which then also turn up in Google searches with different wording.

From topic to calendar: priorities, frequency and resources

At this point you have a list of topics, probably a very long one. The calendar is there to put things in order, taking into account how much you can really produce.

Scoring topics

To rank the list I use a score from 1 to 3 on four criteria: closeness to the service, search interest, ease of ranking, distinctive expertise you can demonstrate. You add up the scores and start with the topics with the highest total. It is not a scientific formula, but it forces you to choose with a criterion rather than on the inspiration of the moment.

For example, a heavily searched topic that is far from the service might total 3 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 7, while a niche question your team knows inside out might reach 1 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 10. The second goes into the calendar first, even though it will bring fewer visits. If the topics are tied to a new service or to a market you are entering, the plan should be aligned with your go-to-market strategy, so that content goes live together with the launch and not six months later.

Choosing a sustainable frequency

Two articles a month for a year are better than eight a month for three months followed by silence. The right frequency is the one the team can keep up without sacrificing quality. Editing time counts too: an article that demonstrates experience often requires an interview or a discussion with someone who knows the subject, and that time needs to go in the calendar.

Scheduling updates

A mature editorial plan does not only include new articles. It sets aside room to update pieces that already perform or that are close to page one. Revising an article sitting in eleventh position often pays off more than writing one from scratch. A simple rule: one slot in three dedicated to updates and consolidation.

A practical example: a six-month plan for a B2B company

Let's take a hypothetical case. An industrial maintenance services company has three lines: scheduled maintenance, emergency call-outs and plant safety consulting. It has one person in marketing who can devote about two days a month to the blog, plus a one-hour monthly interview with the technical director.

The plan for the first six months could be this:

  • Months 1 and 2: the scheduled maintenance area, the most profitable. One pillar ("scheduled maintenance: how to set it up and how much it saves") and two supporting articles on concrete problems.
  • Months 3 and 4: three more articles in the same area, one for the decision stage ("how to evaluate a maintenance supplier"), plus the first update of the pillar based on the searches that emerged.
  • Months 5 and 6: opening of the safety area with a pillar and one supporting article, and a review of the results of the first area.

In total, twelve pieces of work in six months, two a month, with one area completed and one under way. It is not an impressive volume, but each piece has a precise role and links to the right service page. Meanwhile, sales can already use the pillar and the article on choosing a supplier in their deals.

How to measure and review the plan

The editorial plan should be reviewed every quarter. If the only indicator is "how many articles we published", the plan has become a ritual. The useful indicators are different:

  • impressions and average position on the keywords for each topic area;
  • visits that go from articles to the linked service pages;
  • contact requests originating from the blog and their quality;
  • articles close to page one, candidates for an update;
  • topics that bring visits but no click-through to services, to be rethought.

Reading these numbers in a single view makes the quarterly review much faster. A marketing and sales KPI dashboard that connects content, leads and opportunities lets you decide on data rather than impressions. The plan should also be reviewed when business priorities change: if the company launches a new service, the blog has to follow.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Starting from the calendar instead of from services and the customer.
  • Opening too many topic areas at once, without completing any of them.
  • Writing only informational content and ignoring the decision stage.
  • Promising a frequency the team cannot sustain.
  • Leaving no room for updating existing pieces.
  • Disconnecting the blog from sales, who often know customers' questions better than anyone else.

A well-made editorial plan reduces improvisation and creates an archive that grows in value over time. FAQ

How many articles a month does a company blog need?

It depends on your resources, but for an SME two articles a month sustained for a year are a solid base. Consistency matters more than volume.

How often should the editorial plan be reviewed?

At least once a quarter, looking at rankings, click-throughs to service pages and leads. It should also be reviewed when business priorities change or a new service is launched.

What is the difference between pillar content and a supporting article?

The pillar covers a broad topic tied to a service and targets the main keyword. Supporting articles explore specific questions in depth and link back to the pillar.

Who should be involved in the editorial plan?

Marketing, management and sales. Sales in particular knows customers' real questions and objections, which are the best source of topics.
