Strategic business roadmap: how to build one step by step

Reading: about 9 minutes · Updated September 2026

Almost every company I work with has goals. Grow by 20%, enter a new market, improve margins, structure the sales function. Far fewer have a clear answer to the next question: what do we do on Monday morning, and what do we do three months from now, to get there?

A strategic business roadmap exists for exactly this. It turns vague goals into a sequence of initiatives with priorities, timelines, owners and indicators. When it is missing, what I often see happens: the team works on ten fronts at once, nothing really gets finished, and at year end the same goals as the year before are written down again.

In this article I explain the method I use with SMEs and startups to build a roadmap in six steps, with a complete practical example at 90 days and 12 months. At the end you will find the most common mistakes and how to avoid them.

What a strategic business roadmap is (and what it is not)

A strategic roadmap is a map of the few initiatives that take the company from today's goals to those of the next 12 months, ordered in time and linked to each other. It answers four questions: what we do, in what order, who leads it and how we know whether it is working.

To understand it better, it helps to say what it is not:

  • It is not a budget. The budget tells you how much you spend and how much you expect to bring in. The roadmap tells you what you do to achieve those numbers.
  • It is not a project plan. A chart with a hundred tasks is for the people executing. The roadmap is for management to decide and check.
  • It is not a list of ideas. If it contains everything that would be nice to do, it is not choosing anything.

A good roadmap fits on one page. Anyone reading it should understand within a few minutes what the priorities for the quarter are and why.

The six-step method

This is the path I follow when I build a roadmap with a client. It can take from two to six weeks, depending on how orderly the starting data is.

1. Take a snapshot of your starting point

Before deciding where to go, you need to know where you are. Gather a few reliable numbers: revenue and margin by product line or customer segment, the trend over the last 12 to 24 months, the sales pipeline, main costs, team capacity. If this data does not exist or nobody trusts it, the first step of the roadmap is to build it. An initial business audit is designed for exactly this.

2. Set two or three 12-month goals

Goals must be few, numerical and tied to the business. "Improve marketing" is not a goal. "Take new customers from 30 to 50 while keeping gross margin above 35%" is. If there are more than three goals, two of them are usually in conflict and nobody notices.

3. Choose three to five priorities

For each goal ask yourself: what are the two or three levers that really move it? Then cross-check the levers and keep those with the highest impact relative to the effort required. This is where the roadmap becomes strategic, because it forces you to give things up. Everything left out goes on a "not now" list, so nobody brings it back to the table every week.

A simple method I often use: for each lever, score its impact on the goals from 1 to 5 and its ease of execution from 1 to 5, considering the people, time and budget available. Multiply the two scores. For example, "revise the discount policy" might score 4 for impact and 4 for ease, giving 16. "Open a new sales channel abroad" might score 5 for impact but 1 for ease, giving 5. The score does not decide for you, but it makes the choices visible and moves the discussion from opinions to criteria.

4. Turn each priority into initiatives with an owner

Each priority becomes two, three or four concrete initiatives. Each one has a single owner, a start date and an end date, and an expected result. Mark the dependencies: if the new price list first requires the analysis of margin by customer, that analysis comes first.

5. Split time into horizons

The level of detail changes with distance. The first 90 days should be planned month by month. The rest of the year should be planned by quarter, with less detail and more room for correction. Planning twelve months with the same precision is pointless: by July half the plan will have changed.

6. Define indicators and review rhythm

For each priority choose one or two indicators to look at every month. Then put the reviews in the calendar: a one-hour monthly check-in and a broader quarterly review. Without these dates, the roadmap stops existing by the summer.

Practical example: a 90-day and 12-month roadmap

Take a hypothetical company: a B2B distributor of foodservice supplies, with €4 million in revenue, 25 people and about 300 active customers. Over the last two years revenue has stayed flat and gross margin has fallen from 30% to 27%.

After the initial snapshot, three facts emerge. 20% of customers generate 65% of the margin. The sales team works without a defined process and spends most of its time on small customers. Nobody knows the real margin per customer, so discounts are decided case by case.

12-month goals: revenue from €4 million to €4.5 million, gross margin back to 30%, 40 new customers in the hotels and chains segment.

Priorities: 1) control of margin per customer and discount policy; 2) a structured sales process for the hotels and chains segment; 3) more repeat orders from existing customers.

The first 90 days, month by month

  • Month 1. Analysis of margin by customer and by product family. Definition of the ideal customer in the hotels and chains segment. Choice of a CRM or clean-up of the existing one. Owners: management and administration.
  • Month 2. New discount policy with clear thresholds and approval required above a certain percentage. Definition of the sales stages and of the criteria for moving from one stage to the next. List of 150 prospects in the chosen segment.
  • Month 3. Launch of structured outreach to the 150 prospects. First reorder campaign targeting the 60 best customers. First monthly dashboard with margin, pipeline and reorder rate.

The 12 months, quarter by quarter

  • Quarter 1. The foundations described above: margin data, discount policy, sales process, dashboard.
  • Quarter 2. First deals closed in the new segment. Price list review for the lowest-margin product families. Reorder program extended to all mid-tier customers.
  • Quarter 3. Assessment of results: if the hotel segment converts, a second dedicated salesperson is considered. If it does not, the offer is corrected before investing further.
  • Quarter 4. Consolidation, review of the year and setup of the next roadmap.

Monthly indicators: overall gross margin and margin by segment, average discount granted, number of open opportunities in the new segment, customers acquired, reorder rate. Five numbers, not fifty. If you want to build them in an orderly way, I cover this on the page about the KPI dashboard for marketing and sales.

The principle that makes the example work is simple: the first quarter does not aim to sell more, but to build the conditions for doing so. In my experience the first months can still deliver visible results when priorities are few and clear. With a restaurant group we achieved +20% monthly revenue in 2 months.

How to keep the roadmap alive during the year

A roadmap that is never reviewed becomes a historical document. The rhythm I recommend is this:

  • Every month, one hour. You look at the indicators, the status of the initiatives and the blockers. There are three questions: what has been done, what is late and why, what do we decide.
  • Every quarter, half a day. You check whether the priorities are still the right ones, plan the next 90 days in detail and decide what to drop.
  • Once a year, a full review. You start again from the snapshot and rebuild the roadmap for the following year.

The most useful rule I have learned: when a new initiative comes in, one goes out. If everything gets added and nothing removed, within three months the roadmap is back to being a wish list. To connect this work to the financial numbers, management control for marketing and sales is the tool that closes the loop.

Mistakes to avoid

These are the mistakes I see most often when a company tries to build its own roadmap:

  • Too many priorities. If there are ten, they are not priorities. The team treats them as a list and chooses based on the urgency of the day.
  • No single owner. An initiative assigned to "marketing and sales together" is often assigned to nobody.
  • Ignoring team capacity. A roadmap that requires 150% of the available time fails even if every single initiative makes sense.
  • Goals without numbers. "Strengthen the brand" cannot be verified. Choose an indicator, even an imperfect one.
  • Confusing urgencies with priorities. Operational emergencies will always be there. The roadmap exists to protect time for what matters.
  • Writing it alone. If the owner builds it in isolation, the team has it imposed on them. Involving the function heads costs a few more hours and saves months.

FAQ

How many priorities should a strategic roadmap have?

Three to five. With more priorities the team cannot move them all forward and ends up choosing based on the urgencies of the day.

Is a 12-month or a 3-year roadmap better?

For an SME the practical reference is 12 months, with the first 90 days planned in detail. A three-year vision is useful as a direction, but concrete initiatives should be planned over shorter horizons.

How often should the roadmap be updated?

Indicators are checked every month, while priorities are reviewed every quarter. A full review is done once a year.

Who should take part in building the roadmap?

Management and the heads of the functions that will have to execute it. Involving them from the start makes the plan more realistic and easier to carry through.
